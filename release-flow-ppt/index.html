01 Context

What a release is

A release takes a change someone has made and applies it to the servers customers are actually using.

Checkout is down
Out of memory
Developer increases memory
4 GB → 8 GB
Still down
Change isn't applied yet
Release
Applies the change to the live servers
Checkout is back up
Running on 8 GB
Most-used flow in the product

Several times a day, across multiple services.

At real scale

1,000 to 2,000 resources in a single environment at a larger customer.

It changes live infrastructure

Creates, updates and deletes real servers and databases.

No undo

A bad release is fixed with another release, while the product is down.

The story App goes down, usually out of memory. Developer changes 4 gigs to 8. Nothing happens, the site is still down — that's the beat people don't expect. The change exists only on paper. Someone has to run a release to push it to the machines actually running the product, and then checkout comes back up. A release is the step between deciding something and it being true.

Then point at the bottom row: this isn't a rare, careful, once-a-quarter event. Several times a day, two thousand resources, and it can delete a database as easily as restart a service. No undo. That's the flow I redesigned, three times.
02 Problem

The old flow

1

90% of the time users ignored the validation and released anyway. They were warnings. Only errors got fixed.

2

Every decision was crammed into one small modal. The most important one, picking resources, got buried with the rest.

3

No review step before Apply. You never saw what you were about to push.

Questions this raised
Why does a step everyone ignores block the path?
Why is the most important decision buried with the rest?
Why is there no review before production changes?
The framing that matters Validation running first is correct — you're about to change production. The finding is what people did with it: 90% of the time (tracked in Mixpanel) they clicked Ignore & Proceed. Not carelessness. These were warnings, and people knew from experience they weren't what would break the release. Only an actual error made anyone stop and fix. A step that looked critical, blocked the path, and got skipped almost every time. That's why it later became a panel you open, not a door you walk through.

Release type is a real choice that changes every release, so don't call it redundant. Resource groups are a real capability too. The critique is the structure and the step count, not the individual fields.

04 Problem

Evidence

Customer interviews 14 people, 9 orgs
  • No one could say what a release would change. They checked another screen first, then often released a second time to catch what the first one missed.
  • Release lists lived in tickets and Slack, because finding a resource meant typing its exact name.
  • Warnings were ignored on purpose. People had learned which ones never mattered.
  • People caught themselves in the wrong environment mid-release. Nothing asked them to confirm it.
Usage data Mixpanel
  • 90% of validation popups ended with Ignore & Proceed.
  • A successful release meant multiple back-and-forths, not one pass.
  • Search ran on almost every selective release. Nobody browsed the list.
Support tickets

Enhancement requests on the release flow: filters, bulk select, pending-change visibility.

Say this out loud, it matters Releases weren't failing. Every one just carried a detour to check something the flow should have shown, and teams had built their own tracking to cover the gap. Don't overstate this into failed releases — it isn't true and it's the first thing that would get pulled.

Then the turn We were briefed to make the flow shorter. Length was never the problem. People weren't stuck, they were leaving to find what the flow wouldn't show them. So the job wasn't fewer steps, it was showing more.

Confirm before presenting The 90% is Mixpanel-tracked — say so if probed. For the back-and-forth and search lines, know whether you're on a measured figure or a reasonable account. Soften anything you can't back.
05 Problem

Goals

A release asks four things of you. One goal each.

G1 Choose scope
Find what needs releasing without knowing its name

You had to know what to release before you opened the flow.

G2 Understand impact
See what changes before it runs

You couldn't see what a release would do.

G3 Catch problems
Only interrupt when something actually blocks

Warnings interrupted every release and got dismissed every time.

G4 Commit
Know which environment you're in and what you're about to run

You could reach the release button without ever noticing which environment you were in.

Say out loud, not on the slide The business case: deployment frequency up, change failure rate flat, support requests about the release flow down, every customer off the old UI. Two interfaces ran in parallel for about seven months, so migration was the commercial deadline.

How we'd know, if asked G1 filters replace name search · G2 people stop leaving the flow to check · G3 the Ignore & Proceed rate falls · G4 the environment is on screen when the release button is.

Honesty These were directions, not instrumented baselines. Mixpanel behaviour and interviews, no clean before-number. "If I ran this again I'd instrument before designing."
06 Decisions

Version 1 Old UI

G1 partly G2 G3 G4
What I did

I wasn't allowed to change the flow, so I worked inside the dropdown. Two filters, All Resource and Release Pending, a pending marker on every row, and the resource type as an icon.

The cost

Everything outside the dropdown stayed as it was — no preview, the same validation dialog, the environment still named once at the top.

Where it landed

Dropped. It clears one goal of four, and a dropdown is the wrong control for choosing from a thousand resources.

Cover in Figma if asked The tooltip carrying name, intent and flavour — detail the dropdown was otherwise hiding.

The beat "It genuinely helped. It wasn't enough, and I knew it while I was drawing it."
07 Decisions

Version 2 Old UI

G1 G2 G3 partly G4
What I did

Redesigned the flow as three steps. Settings, then the dropdown as a filterable table, then a review step showing what each resource would change.

The cost

More steps than people had before, and changing your mind in step two sent you back to step one.

Where it landed

Cut to the table plus the review step. Never built, because everything new was going into React, but that scope became the spec.

Cover in Figma Two variants at step two. One goes straight to review, the other adds Edit config & Release and opens the full resource config form inside the flow. I killed that one — config runs past a hundred fields on some resources and has a JSON mode people prefer, and editing and releasing happen at different times.

The beat "I'd solved one thing and made the flow heavier doing it." Then: most return, least disruption, no new flow for anyone to learn.

Version 3 React

G1 G2 G3 G4
What I did

One full panel instead of a modal, split by weight. Selecting resources on the right because that's the actual work. Release type, settings and validation in the left rail.

The cost

A routine one-resource release gets slower, because review is a step of its own.

Where it landed

Shipped. Simplified twice on the way — validation became a tag, and change detail moved out of selection into review.

Cover in Figma Three things I drew and dropped. A Start a release modal for picking release type, killed as an unnecessary step, its content kept as a tooltip. 11 Solution

Edge cases

A resource has a blocking error
The row can't be selected, and the tooltip says why, with a link to the validation.
Something is blocked and you pick full release
Full release is disabled, so a broken resource can't ride in on the coarser option. Select all in a selective release ships everything else.
A release failed
It's in release history. Re-releasing from there comes back as a selective release with the same resources already picked.
You don't have permission
The option is visible but disabled, and names the permission you're missing.
A resource is disabled
It stays in the list with no change types. Take it out and you've told someone their resource is gone, so they go looking for something that's still there.
Go deep on two, don't read five Blocking errors and permissions. Every string is literal from ReleaseOptionsDrawer.tsx :543, :546, :620-621 — all demoable live.

The rule under all of them, say it out loud Disable and explain, never hide. On infrastructure a missing option reads as a bug and becomes a support ticket. A disabled one with a reason answers the question where it comes up.

How each number was got, say it before they ask 70s to 33s and 2.3 to 1.1 re-attempts: I timed the same tasks myself, before and after. A consistent before-and-after run by one person, not instrumentation. 9% is a new measure — there was no equivalent before, because the old flow interrupted every release rather than letting one through.

The line that makes the 9% land On the evidence slide you said 90% of validation popups ended with Ignore & Proceed. Don't present 90 to 9 as one delta, the denominators differ. Say it as: the interrupt fired on everything and got dismissed nine times in ten; now nine percent of releases carry a warning past the line and the rest have nothing to dismiss.

Struck on purpose UI-attributable failure rate 34% to 12% is off this slide. SESSION-CONTEXT §5 says no releases failed because of the UI, and pm-review names that exact figure as one that was already wrong. Put it back only if you can point at a release that failed because of the interface.

Held in reserve Satisfaction 5.8 to 8.4 on the same 14-person cohort. You scored the responses yourself, so it's a consistent before-and-after on one cohort, not a validated instrument. Use it only if they push for user sentiment.
